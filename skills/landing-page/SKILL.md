# SKILL.md — Google Ads Landing Page Builder

## Purpose

Use this skill to plan, write, and structure profitable Google Ads-specific landing pages instead of sending every click to a generic product detail page or collection page.

The goal is to match the landing page to the buyer's search intent, awareness level, traffic temperature, and decision stage.

## Core Principle

Do not send all Google Ads traffic to the same product page.

Product pages work best for bottom-of-funnel buyers who already know what they want and are ready to purchase.

For search, informational, comparison, competitor, and cold traffic, build dedicated landing pages that educate, frame the problem, handle objections, and make the product feel like the logical solution.

## When Product Pages Are Not Enough

Use a dedicated landing page when:

- The searcher needs education before buying.
- The query is informational or problem-aware.
- The buyer is comparing options.
- The buyer is considering a competitor.
- The product has a higher AOV.
- The product needs proof, explanation, expert validation, or social proof.
- The search intent is too specific for a generic product page.
- The traffic is cold or semi-warm.

Do not rely only on product pages for queries like:

- “best supplements to lose weight”
- “how to lose weight after 50”
- “[competitor] alternative”
- “best [product category]”
- “[product] for [use case]”
- “why am I still dealing with [problem]”

## Landing Page Format Selection Rules

Choose the page format based on the intent behind the traffic.

### 1. Keyword Theme Pages

Use this when the query contains a clear product + benefit, product + use case, or product + audience angle.

Best for:

- “[product] + benefit”
- “[product] for [use case]”
- “[product] for [audience]”

Do:

- Build one page variation per buyer type, use case, or search theme.
- Match the headline directly to the query.
- Rewrite product titles and above-the-fold copy around the keyword theme.
- Start with best-selling products and create 1–2 test pages before scaling.
- Use separate ad groups for each keyword theme.
- Keep the product central, but frame it through the searcher's specific problem.

Do not:

- Send every theme to one generic product page.
- Use the same headline for all buyer types.
- Build pages for themes with no real search volume.

Example:

If selling magnesium supplements, create different pages for:

- magnesium for sleep
- magnesium for anxiety
- magnesium for muscle recovery
- magnesium for kids

Each buyer has a different problem. One generic product page cannot speak to all of them well.

### 2. Advertorials

Use this when the buyer is problem-aware but not yet solution-ready.

Best for:

- “how to fix [problem]”
- “does [solution] work”
- “why am I still dealing with [problem]”
- “what causes [problem]”
- “best way to solve [problem]”

Do:

- Make the page read like editorial content first.
- Educate before selling.
- Introduce the product only after the problem is clearly explained.
- Support claims with studies, expert opinions, data, or cited proof.
- Keep the CTA subtle early in the page.
- Increase CTA strength as the reader moves down the page.
- Use standard search campaigns, dynamic search ads, or Demand Gen to test angles.

Do not:

- Make the page look like a hard-sell product page from the first screen.
- Lead with discounts before the reader understands the problem.
- Make unsupported claims.
- Use aggressive sales language too early.

Recommended structure:

1. Problem-led headline
2. Short intro that confirms the reader's pain
3. Explanation of why the problem happens
4. Common failed solutions
5. New mechanism or better approach
6. Product introduced as the solution
7. Proof
8. Objection handling
9. CTA

### 3. Listicles

Use this when the searcher wants options, mistakes, reasons, or solutions in a scannable format.

Best for:

- “reasons for [problem]”
- “mistakes people make with [topic]”
- “how to solve [problem]”
- “best ways to [outcome]”
- “things to know before buying [product category]”

Do:

- Make the article easy to scan.
- Deliver the number promised in the headline.
- Use the product as one of the solutions, not the obvious pitch from the beginning.
- Place the product naturally inside the list.
- Use the same traffic strategies as advertorials: search campaigns, DSA, and Demand Gen.
- Keep formatting simple: short sections, strong subheads, bullets, and clear takeaways.

Do not:

- Promise “7 reasons” and only explain 4 properly.
- Make every list item secretly point to the product.
- Hide the sales intent so much that the reader never understands what to do next.

Recommended structure:

1. Numbered headline
2. Short explanation of who the article is for
3. List item 1–2: education/problem
4. List item 3–5: mistakes or alternatives
5. Product appears as a logical solution
6. Proof and comparison
7. CTA

### 4. Us vs. Them Pages

Use this when the buyer is comparing your product against a specific competitor or known alternative.

Best for:

- “[competitor name]”
- “[competitor] alternative”
- “[competitor] review”
- “[your product] vs [competitor]”
- “alternatives to [competitor]”

Do:

- Explain why the comparison exists before showing features.
- Keep the page factual.
- Highlight your strengths without trashing the competitor.
- Add a visual comparison table.
- Include testimonials from buyers who switched.
- Use independent review style when comparing with smaller competitors.
- Use safer branded-page framing when comparing against a larger brand.
- Target competitor brand terms with dedicated search campaigns where legally and platform-policy appropriate.
- Use custom segments and audience signals in Performance Max where appropriate.

Do not:

- Make false or unverifiable claims.
- Use competitor trademarks in misleading ways.
- Attack the competitor.
- Ignore cases where the competitor may be a better fit.
- Compare only features; compare outcomes, fit, proof, support, pricing, and use cases.

Recommended structure:

1. Comparison headline
2. Why this comparison matters
3. Who each product is best for
4. Feature comparison table
5. Where your product wins
6. Where the competitor may be better
7. Switching proof
8. CTA

### 5. Third-Party Comparison Pages

Use this when the buyer wants a ranked list or category-level evaluation.

Best for:

- “best [product category]”
- “top [product] for [use case]”
- “[product] reviews”
- “[competitor] alternative”
- “best [category] for [audience]”

Do:

- Frame the page as a category guide, not a direct two-product comparison.
- Explain how the list was created before the rankings.
- Be transparent about evaluation criteria.
- Include real pros and cons for every product, including your own.
- Write in a neutral tone.
- Use search campaigns for category and competitor keywords.
- Use Google Discover or Demand Gen-style native creative when the content is listicle-like.
- Make the content useful even if the reader does not buy immediately.

Do not:

- Rank your product first without explaining why.
- Pretend to be neutral while obviously hiding bias.
- Include fake reviews or unverifiable claims.
- Only criticize competitors.
- Hide weaknesses of your own product.

Recommended structure:

1. Category-focused headline
2. Who this guide is for
3. How products were evaluated
4. Ranked list
5. Pros and cons for each option
6. Best fit by use case
7. Final recommendation
8. CTA

### 6. Sales Pages

Use this when the product needs deeper persuasion before the buyer converts.

Best for:

- Higher-AOV products
- Complex products
- Skeptical niches
- Supplements, beauty, health-adjacent, and transformation-based products
- Products requiring education before purchase
- Cold traffic from Shopping, Demand Gen, YouTube, or broad search

Do:

- Cover the problem, mechanism, benefits, proof, and offer in one page.
- Match the page angle to the product title, ad creative, and product feed.
- Test sales pages against normal product pages.
- Optimize for mobile first.
- Use proof throughout the page: testimonials, studies, experts, media, before/after, demonstrations, and FAQs.
- Link back to the product multiple times.
- Use stronger CTAs after proof sections.
- Duplicate your feed to compare PDP traffic against sales-page traffic.

Do not:

- Treat cold traffic like ready-to-buy traffic.
- Put all proof near the bottom.
- Make the page too desktop-oriented.
- Use vague benefit claims.
- Forget to restate the offer multiple times.

Recommended structure:

1. Problem-aware headline
2. Emotional problem framing
3. New mechanism or unique insight
4. Product introduced as the solution
5. Benefits
6. Proof
7. Objection handling
8. Offer
9. FAQ
10. Final CTA

## Traffic Matching Rules

Match traffic type to landing page type.

### Search Campaigns

Use for:

- Keyword theme pages
- Advertorials
- Listicles
- Us vs. Them pages
- Third-party comparison pages
- Sales pages targeting long-tail intent

Instructions:

- Build tightly matched ad groups.
- Match ad copy to landing page headline.
- Use exact and phrase match first for testing.
- Expand only after page-level conversion data is clear.

### Shopping Campaigns

Use for:

- Keyword theme pages
- Sales pages
- Product-specific pages

Instructions:

- Rewrite product feed titles to match the page theme.
- Duplicate feeds to test sales-page traffic against PDP traffic.
- Segment high-AOV or complex products separately.

### Dynamic Search Ads

Use for:

- Advertorials
- Listicles
- Content-style landing pages

Instructions:

- Target specific URL rules such as `/blog/`, `/guides/`, or `/articles/`.
- Use DSA to discover unexpected search terms.
- Move winning queries into standard search campaigns.

### Demand Gen / YouTube

Use for:

- Advertorials
- Listicles
- Sales pages
- Transformation or hidden-problem angles

Instructions:

- Use creatives that surface a problem or before/after transformation.
- Send traffic to educational pages first.
- Do not send cold Demand Gen traffic directly to a thin PDP.

### Performance Max

Use for:

- Us vs. Them pages
- Sales pages
- Audience-signal testing
- Competitor-alternative positioning

Instructions:

- Build custom segments around competitor names, category searches, and buyer-intent terms.
- Use dedicated assets that match the landing page angle.
- Separate experimental landing-page tests from core product campaigns where possible.

## Page Copy Rules

Every landing page should answer these questions:

1. What problem does the visitor have?
2. Why has the problem not been solved yet?
3. What does the visitor currently believe?
4. What does the page need to reframe?
5. Why is this product or offer the logical next step?
6. What proof removes skepticism?
7. What objections will block conversion?
8. What CTA fits the visitor's awareness level?

## Above-the-Fold Rules

The first screen should include:

- A headline that matches the search intent
- A subheadline that explains the outcome or reason to continue
- A visual or product/context image
- One primary CTA
- One proof element, such as reviews, expert mention, press, statistic, or customer result

Do not waste the first screen with generic brand messaging.

## Proof Rules

Use proof based on the buyer's skepticism level.

For low skepticism:

- Reviews
- Ratings
- Simple testimonials
- Product benefits

For medium skepticism:

- Before/after examples
- Demonstrations
- Comparisons
- Expert commentary
- Specific customer stories

For high skepticism:

- Studies
- Clinical or technical explanations
- Detailed mechanisms
- Long-form testimonials
- Objection-specific proof
- Third-party validation

## CTA Rules

Match the CTA to the traffic temperature.

For cold traffic:

- “See how it works”
- “Find the right option”
- “Read the guide”
- “Compare options”

For warm traffic:

- “Shop now”
- “Choose your plan”
- “Get started”
- “See best sellers”

For hot traffic:

- “Buy now”
- “Claim offer”
- “Start today”
- “Add to cart”

## Testing Sequence

Start with the highest-leverage page format based on current traffic.

1. Identify the top 5–10 search terms by spend, volume, or missed opportunity.
2. Categorize each query by intent.
3. Choose the matching landing page format.
4. Build one page for the strongest angle.
5. Match ads and headlines exactly to the page angle.
6. Run the test against the existing product page.
7. Measure CVR, CPA, ROAS, AOV, scroll depth, CTA clicks, and assisted conversions.
8. Keep the winner.
9. Duplicate the winning structure into adjacent themes.

## Metrics to Track

Track:

- Conversion rate
- Cost per acquisition
- Return on ad spend
- Average order value
- Add-to-cart rate
- CTA click rate
- Scroll depth
- Time on page
- Bounce rate
- Search term quality
- Assisted conversions
- New customer acquisition cost

Do not judge landing pages only by direct last-click conversions. Educational pages may assist conversions across multiple sessions.

## Common Mistakes

Avoid:

- Sending all traffic to one product page
- Using the same page for every buyer type
- Building pages without matching ad groups
- Making advertorials look like obvious product pages
- Making comparison pages too aggressive
- Writing listicles that do not deliver the promised list
- Ignoring mobile layout
- Hiding proof below the fold
- Using weak or generic CTAs
- Scaling before testing one strong page

## Final Rule

The landing page must match the visitor's intent more closely than a generic product page can.

If the visitor needs education, build education.
If the visitor needs comparison, build comparison.
If the visitor needs proof, build proof.
If the visitor is ready to buy, make buying easy.
